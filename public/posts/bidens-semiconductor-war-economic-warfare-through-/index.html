Biden's Semiconductor War: Economic Warfare Through Tech Controls

March 27, 2026

The semiconductor export controls that Biden unleashed on China in October 2022 weren’t just tech policy—they were economic warfare disguised as national security. While mainstream media focuses on chip shortages and AI competition, the real game is playing out in third-country manufacturing hubs, corporate balance sheets, and the global debt markets that finance Asia’s industrial base.

The amateur sees this as America trying to slow China’s tech development. The strategist recognizes this as the opening move in a systematic campaign to restructure global supply chains—with profound implications for every economy caught between the two superpowers.

The Numbers Behind the Narrative#

The scale of this economic disruption dwarfs public understanding. China imported $415 billion worth of semiconductors in 2021—more than oil. The October controls didn’t just target individual companies; they created a blanket ban on any semiconductor equipment capable of producing chips at 14 nanometers or below.

Here’s what that actually means: Applied Materials, the largest semiconductor equipment manufacturer, saw its China revenue drop from $7.9 billion in 2022 to $4.6 billion in 2023—a 42% collapse in a single year. KLA Corporation’s China business fell from $2.9 billion to $1.8 billion over the same period.

But the real damage is in the multiplier effects. Every $1 of semiconductor equipment typically supports $10-15 in downstream manufacturing. Apply that ratio to the $20+ billion in equipment sales that vanished, and you’re looking at $200-300 billion in disrupted economic activity.

The controls also triggered something Beijing hadn’t fully anticipated: capital flight from Chinese semiconductor startups. Venture funding for Chinese chip companies fell 63% year-over-year in 2023, from $11.8 billion to $4.4 billion, according to PitchBook data.

The Chess Board: White Forces the Play#

This isn’t a bilateral trade dispute—it’s a systematic encirclement strategy that forces every major economy to choose sides. The U.S. is playing white, setting the tempo and forcing reactions across multiple fronts simultaneously.

The brilliance lies in the execution mechanism. Rather than imposing tariffs that invite retaliation, the controls target the chokepoints of production itself. ASML, the Dutch company that makes the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines essential for advanced chips, became the unwitting enforcer of American policy when the Netherlands government blocked its China sales in 2023.

Tokyo followed suit, with Japan’s export restrictions on semiconductor materials taking effect in July 2023. The result: China’s access to the tools for advanced chip production didn’t just slow—it effectively terminated for cutting-edge processes.

Beijing finds itself playing black, constantly reacting. Xi Jinping’s government announced a $143 billion semiconductor development fund in response, but money can’t solve the fundamental problem: China needs the machines it can no longer buy, and building indigenous alternatives will take 8-10 years minimum.

The Motive: Beyond Tech Competition#

Follow the money, and the real strategy emerges. This isn’t primarily about slowing Chinese AI development—that’s the public justification. The actual goal is supply chain onshoring that benefits specific American constituencies.

Intel, which spent decades moving production offshore, suddenly becomes the primary beneficiary of the $52 billion CHIPS Act subsidies. The company is building massive fabs in Ohio and Arizona, positioning itself as the “trusted” alternative to Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) for sensitive applications.

Meanwhile, private equity and sovereign wealth funds are quietly restructuring the global semiconductor landscape. KKR raised a $19 billion infrastructure fund in 2023, with significant allocations to semiconductor manufacturing facilities in “friend-shoring” locations like Vietnam and India.

The defense contractors are equally strategic. Raytheon and Lockheed Martin have quietly lobbied for expanded definitions of “dual-use” technology, broadening the scope of export controls to include components in everything from 5G base stations to electric vehicle controllers.

The hidden winner: American semiconductor equipment companies, which now face reduced competition for non-China business while benefiting from reshoring subsidies. Applied Materials’ gross margins improved 340 basis points in 2023 as supply tightened.

Third-Order Effects: The Real Disruption#

The mainstream narrative misses where the actual economic damage is concentrating. China represented 35% of global semiconductor demand in 2021. When you suddenly cut off that market, the effects cascade through seemingly unrelated sectors.

Vietnam’s Semiconductor Assembly Boom: Vietnam’s electronics exports surged 8.7% in 2023, driven largely by companies like Foxconn relocating assembly operations from China. The country is emerging as the primary beneficiary of supply chain diversification, with foreign direct investment in electronics reaching $6.8 billion—a 34% increase year-over-year.

South Korea’s Impossible Position: Samsung and SK Hynix face an existential dilemma. China accounted for 40% of Samsung’s semiconductor revenue in 2022. The company received one-year waivers to continue operating its China fabs, but those expire in October 2024. Samsung’s share price has underperformed the broader Korean market by 15% since the controls were announced, as investors price in permanent market share loss.

The Debt Cascade: Here’s the effect nobody’s tracking systematically. Chinese semiconductor companies collectively hold over $85 billion in dollar-denominated debt, much of it tied to equipment purchases that are now impossible. As revenue projections collapse, refinancing becomes prohibitively expensive. This is creating a slow-motion debt crisis in China’s tech sector.

Historical Parallel: The COCOM Playbook#

This strategy has precedent, and it worked before. The Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls (COCOM), established in 1949, used similar technology export restrictions to constrain Soviet industrial development throughout the Cold War.

The key insight from COCOM: the restrictions succeeded not by preventing all technology transfer, but by imposing massive time delays and cost overruns on Soviet projects. The USSR could eventually reverse-engineer Western technology, but by the time they succeeded, the West had moved two generations ahead.

The semiconductor controls follow the same playbook with 21st-century precision. China will eventually develop indigenous EUV lithography, but ASML’s current machines represent 20+ years of accumulated know-how. By the time China catches up to today’s technology, the cutting edge will have moved far ahead.

The difference: modern semiconductor development requires far more capital than Cold War-era technology programs. China’s $143 billion fund sounds massive until you realize TSMC alone invested $36 billion in R&D and capex in 2023. Beijing is trying to catch up to an industry that collectively spends over $200 billion annually on advancement.

The Corporate Response: Quiet Compliance#

Public statements from affected companies emphasize compliance and resilience, but private actions tell a different story. Nvidia, despite losing a $5 billion annual China business overnight, has quietly shifted production of its data center GPUs to facilities that can serve non-Chinese markets exclusively.

The more revealing signal: executive compensation structures at major semiconductor companies are increasingly tied to metrics that exclude China revenue. Applied Materials restructured its executive bonuses in 2023 to focus on “strategic market” performance—effectively accepting permanent China market share loss.

TSMC’s response has been the most sophisticated. The company is building advanced fabs in Arizona while simultaneously maintaining its China customer relationships through careful product segmentation. TSMC’s trailing-edge processes (28nm and above) remain China-accessible, while cutting-edge production (5nm and below) is reserved for “trusted” customers.

What to Watch#

The next phase of this economic conflict will be determined by three specific developments:

October 2024 Waiver Expirations: Samsung and SK Hynix must choose between their China operations and continued access to U.S. technology. Watch for announcements about fab closures or technology transfers in Q4 2024.

China’s Indigenous EUV Timeline: Beijing claims it will achieve domestic EUV capability by 2026. Monitor patent filings and talent acquisition by Chinese lithography companies like Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment (SMEE). Any breakthrough here would fundamentally alter the strategic calculation.

Third-Country Manufacturing Investment Flows: Track FDI data from Vietnam, Malaysia, and India. These countries are becoming the primary beneficiaries of supply chain restructuring. Sudden spikes in electronics-sector investment will signal where production is actually moving.

Corporate Debt Refinancing in Chinese Tech: Watch for restructuring announcements from heavily leveraged Chinese semiconductor companies in 2024. The first major defaults will trigger broader financial contagion in China’s tech sector.

The semiconductor war isn’t a trade dispute—it’s industrial policy by other means. The country that controls advanced chip production will dominate the next decade of economic growth. Everything else is just commentary.
