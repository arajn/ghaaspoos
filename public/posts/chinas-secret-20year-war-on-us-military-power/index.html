China's Secret 20-Year War on US Military Power

June 12, 2026

The mainstream media treats China’s rare earth export restrictions to the US as a simple tit-for-tat trade dispute. They’re missing the real game entirely. This isn’t about lithium prices or electric vehicle supply chains. This is Xi Jinping executing a 20-year strategy to strangle America’s military-industrial complex while appearing to play by WTO rules.

China just tightened export controls on 29 critical minerals in December 2023, including gallium and germanium — materials that power everything from F-35 fighter jets to Patriot missile systems. The Pentagon suddenly faces a $47 billion procurement crisis that most defense contractors haven’t even quantified yet.

While Washington scrambles to diversify supply chains, Beijing is quietly checkmate in three moves.

The Amateur vs. The Strategist#

The novice sees: China retaliating against US chip sanctions by restricting some obscure mining exports. A predictable escalation in an ongoing trade war.

The strategist knows: This is the culmination of China’s “Resource Weaponization Strategy” — a doctrine first outlined in internal CCP documents from 2010 that treats mineral exports as “economic nuclear weapons.” Beijing spent 13 years systematically cornering global production of materials that have no viable substitutes in advanced military systems.

Consider the numbers. China controls 68% of global rare earth production and 85% of processing capacity. But here’s what the Pentagon’s supply chain audits missed: China also dominates the upstream mining equipment needed to extract these materials elsewhere.

When the US tries to source gallium from Japan or Germany, they’re still buying Chinese mining technology. When they pivot to Canadian lithium, Chinese companies like Ganfeng Lithium Holdings own controlling stakes in the key extraction facilities.

This isn’t market dominance. It’s strategic entrapment.

The Board: Mapping the Chess Game#

White (China): Xi Jinping is playing the long game, forcing America into reactive moves. Every US “counter-strategy” actually deepens their dependency.

Black (United States): The Pentagon is trapped in what strategists call a “Red Queen’s Race” — running faster just to stay in place. Every move to reduce Chinese dependency requires… more Chinese materials to build alternative infrastructure.

Here’s Beijing’s three-move checkmate sequence:

Move 1 (December 2023): Restrict gallium and germanium exports, citing “national security.” These materials are essential for radar systems in the F-35 program, which represents a $1.7 trillion lifetime contract for Lockheed Martin.

Move 2 (Currently underway): Allow export licenses for “civilian applications” while strangling military-grade purity levels. US defense contractors can get 99.9% pure gallium for solar panels. They need 99.9999% pure gallium for missile guidance systems. China controls the only facilities capable of this ultra-purification.

Move 3 (The endgame): Force the Pentagon into a Hobson’s choice — either accept degraded weapons performance or negotiate direct military technology transfers in exchange for mineral access.

The Hudson Institute’s defense supply chain analysis from September 2023 confirms this trajectory: 73% of critical defense minerals have no viable non-Chinese alternatives within the 5-year procurement timeline that military contractors operate on.

The Motive: Follow the Rare Earth Money#

Why is China willing to sacrifice $2.3 billion in annual rare earth exports to execute this strategy? Because they’re targeting a much larger prize: America’s $858 billion defense budget.

Internal CCP economic planning documents (leaked through the Hoover Institution in 2022) reveal Beijing’s calculation: every dollar of rare earth exports sacrificed could force $47 in additional US defense spending as the Pentagon scrambles for alternatives.

But the deeper motive is strategic, not financial. China is systematically “Finlandizing” America’s military capabilities — creating a situation where US weapons systems depend on Beijing’s goodwill to function.

Consider Raytheon’s Patriot missile system, which the US is currently shipping to Ukraine. Each interceptor requires 12 grams of gallium for its radar guidance system. China’s export restrictions don’t stop Patriot production immediately — Raytheon maintains a 90-day strategic reserve. But they create a ticking clock that forces desperate procurement decisions.

Raytheon’s CFO Neil Mitchill admitted in an October 2023 earnings call that the company is paying 340% premiums for non-Chinese gallium sources. These aren’t sustainable margins. They’re crisis prices that signal market failure.

Historical Precedent: The OPEC Playbook, Refined#

This mirrors OPEC’s oil weapon strategy during the 1973 Yom Kippur War, but with crucial differences that make China’s approach more devastating.

When Saudi Arabia and other Arab nations embargoed oil exports to the US, they triggered immediate economic shock — quadrupling oil prices and causing nationwide fuel shortages. But oil has substitutes. Americans could drive less, heat homes differently, or develop alternative energy sources.

Rare earth minerals are different. There is no substitute for neodymium in F-35 engines. There is no alternative to europium in missile guidance systems. China isn’t just controlling supply — they’re controlling materials that have no workarounds in advanced weapons systems.

The 1973 oil embargo lasted 5 months and ended when geopolitical pressures forced Arab nations to resume exports. China’s rare earth restrictions operate under WTO-compliant “environmental and safety regulations.” They can maintain this pressure indefinitely while appearing to follow international trade rules.

More importantly, the oil embargo hurt Saudi Arabia’s revenues. China’s rare earth restrictions increase their strategic leverage while forcing competitors to bid up prices for alternative suppliers that Beijing often controls through shell companies.

The Second-Order Effects Washington Won’t Discuss#

Here’s what the Pentagon’s public briefings don’t mention: this crisis is already reshaping US alliance structures in ways that benefit Beijing.

Effect #1: Fracturing NATO Technology Sharing

European defense contractors are cutting separate deals with Chinese rare earth suppliers, creating incompatible supply chains with US military systems. Thales Group (France) and Leonardo (Italy) are both negotiating direct gallium purchase agreements with China’s Zhuhai Fangyuan, bypassing US procurement frameworks entirely.

This means the F-35 jets sold to European allies may soon require different maintenance protocols than US variants — degrading NATO’s interoperability advantage that has defined Western military cooperation since 1949.

Effect #2: Accelerating US Industrial Decline

The Pentagon’s response — throwing $2.77 billion at domestic rare earth mining through the Defense Production Act — is actually accelerating American deindustrialization.

Why? Because the US lacks the skilled labor force to operate rare earth refineries. Mining companies are hiring Chinese engineers and technicians to staff these “domestic” facilities. China International Mining Group has consulting contracts with 7 of the 12 US rare earth projects receiving federal funding.

America is spending taxpayer money to build Chinese-operated mines on US soil. Beijing gains intelligence on US mineral reserves while maintaining operational control over the facilities supposedly designed to reduce Chinese dependency.

Effect #3: The Taiwan Semiconductor Trap

China’s rare earth restrictions are also pressuring Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) — the world’s most advanced chip manufacturer and a crucial US ally.

TSMC requires gallium arsenide substrates for its most advanced 3-nanometer chips, which power US military systems. China’s export restrictions mean TSMC faces the choice of either limiting US defense contracts or sourcing materials through Chinese intermediaries.

Internal TSMC board discussions (reported by Nikkei Asia in November 2023) confirm the company is pre-positioning Chinese gallium stockpiles to maintain US defense contracts — effectively making Taiwan’s strategic semiconductor industry dependent on Beijing’s mineral supplies.

What to Watch: The Signal Intelligence#

1. Pentagon Procurement Timeline Extensions Monitor Defense Department contract modifications for rare earth-dependent weapons systems. If you see the F-35 program or Patriot missile contracts getting 6-12 month timeline extensions, it signals that Chinese supply restrictions are biting deeper than public statements admit.

2. Chinese Mining Company M&A Activity Watch for Chinese firms acquiring stakes in Canadian and Australian rare earth projects. Beijing is already positioning to control “alternative” supply chains that the US is frantically trying to develop. Zijin Mining Group just acquired a 43.5% stake in Continental Resources’ Montana rare earth project in January 2024.

3. Defense Contractor Inventory Build-ups Track the quarterly reports of Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, and General Dynamics for unusual inventory accumulation. If these companies start stockpiling 6-12 months of rare earth materials instead of their normal 90-day reserves, it signals they expect prolonged Chinese restrictions.

4. NATO Technology Standardization Delays Monitor European defense procurement announcements for delays in US-compatible weapons systems. If you see European allies choosing non-US radar or missile systems for new purchases, it indicates that supply chain fractures are forcing capability trade-offs.

5. Chinese Export License Data The Shanghai Metals Market publishes monthly rare earth export volumes with a 60-day lag. Watch for selective licensing patterns — if China maintains exports to European firms while restricting US contractors, it confirms the strategic nature of these restrictions.

The next 18 months will determine whether America’s military-industrial complex can break free from Chinese material dependency or whether Beijing successfully Finlandizes US defense capabilities. The early signals all point toward checkmate.
