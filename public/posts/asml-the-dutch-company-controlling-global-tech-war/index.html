ASML: The Dutch Company Controlling Global Tech Wars

April 29, 2026

The world’s most advanced fighter jets, smartphones, and data centers all depend on a single Dutch company that most people have never heard of. ASML Holding produces every single extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machine on Earth — the $200 million marvels that etch circuits smaller than a virus onto silicon wafers.

Here’s what the headlines miss: ASML isn’t just a supplier. It’s the ultimate geopolitical weapon, and the U.S. just weaponized it against China in ways that will reshape global technology for decades.

The Monopoly No One Talks About#

The amateur sees semiconductor shortages and thinks about car production delays. The strategist knows this is actually about who controls the commanding heights of the digital economy.

ASML shipped just 31 EUV machines in Q3 2023, down from 42 in Q2. Each machine weighs 180 tons, requires three Boeing 747s to transport, and takes 40,000 specialized components from 5,000 suppliers across 40 countries. The precision required? These machines must hit targets 25,000 times smaller than the width of a human hair while operating in a perfect vacuum.

Only ASML can build them. Not Intel. Not Samsung. Not the entire Chinese semiconductor industry combined.

The company controls 100% of the EUV market and 90% of all lithography equipment globally. In 2023, ASML generated €27.6 billion in revenue — more than the GDP of Estonia. Yet most business leaders couldn’t identify CEO Peter Wennink in a lineup.

This isn’t an accident. It’s the result of a 40-year engineering marathon that created the most sophisticated chokepoint in modern manufacturing.

The Physics of Control#

EUV lithography operates at the bleeding edge of what’s physically possible. The machines generate light at 13.5 nanometers — so energetic it gets absorbed by air itself. The entire process happens in a vacuum chamber where molten tin droplets get blasted by a 25-kilowatt laser 50,000 times per second, creating plasma hotter than the surface of the sun.

Here’s the strategic reality: without EUV machines, you cannot manufacture chips smaller than 7 nanometers at scale. And without sub-7nm chips, you cannot build competitive AI processors, 5G infrastructure, or advanced military systems.

TSMC, the world’s largest contract chipmaker, operates 160+ EUV machines across its fabs in Taiwan. Each machine can process about 1,500 wafers per day, generating roughly $2 million in revenue daily. Do the math: ASML’s installed base at TSMC alone generates over $100 billion annually.

Samsung runs approximately 50 EUV machines. Intel operates around 30. China’s leading foundry, SMIC, has zero next-generation EUV systems — and under current export restrictions, will never get them.

The Chessboard: White Moves First#

The novice thinks export controls are about preventing China from buying specific products. The chess master recognizes this as a systematic decoupling designed to lock in technological advantage for decades.

In October 2022, the Biden administration implemented the most sweeping semiconductor export controls in history. The restrictions don’t just block EUV sales to China — they prohibit American citizens from supporting Chinese semiconductor development, create “foreign direct product” rules that block non-U.S. companies using American technology, and require licenses for any equipment that might be used in advanced chip production.

ASML immediately suspended all EUV shipments and support to Chinese customers. The company’s China revenue dropped from €2.16 billion in 2021 to €1.09 billion in 2023 — a 50% collapse representing roughly 8,000 high-paying engineering jobs that will never return.

But here’s the hidden move: the U.S. didn’t just cut off China’s access to the most advanced tools. It forced China to rebuild the entire semiconductor stack from scratch, setting Beijing back at least a decade.

Historical Parallel: The COCOM Precedent#

This playbook isn’t new. During the Cold War, the U.S. led the Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls (COCOM), which restricted technology exports to Soviet bloc countries. The controls covered everything from advanced computing equipment to precision manufacturing tools.

The results were devastating for Soviet technological development. While American companies like IBM and Intel pushed the boundaries of semiconductor design, Soviet engineers were forced to reverse-engineer Western chips using inferior domestic tooling. By 1989, Soviet microprocessors lagged Western equivalents by 5-10 years.

The difference today is scale and speed. Modern semiconductor development cycles compress decades of innovation into 18-month product cycles. A 3-year technology gap in 1985 meant slower computers. A 3-year gap in 2024 means falling behind in artificial intelligence, autonomous vehicles, and military systems that determine national security.

China’s leadership understands this dynamic perfectly. Beijing allocated $143 billion to domestic semiconductor development through the National IC Industry Investment Fund. Yet despite massive capital deployment, China’s most advanced domestic foundry, SMIC, remains stuck at 14nm production — roughly five technology generations behind TSMC’s 3nm process.

The Money Trail: Why ASML Benefits#

Here’s what the mainstream narrative gets wrong: export restrictions don’t hurt ASML — they cement the company’s strategic position.

ASML’s order backlog hit a record €38 billion in Q3 2023, representing roughly 18 months of production. The company raised its 2030 revenue guidance to €60 billion, implying 8% annual growth despite losing the Chinese market entirely.

How is this possible? Simple economics. Artificial scarcity increases pricing power.

With Chinese demand permanently severed, ASML can prioritize shipments to TSMC, Samsung, and Intel — customers willing to pay premium prices for guaranteed supply. The company raised EUV machine prices by 15% in 2023 while extending delivery timelines to 12+ months.

Meanwhile, ASML’s technology moat continues widening. The company spends €3 billion annually on R&D — more than most countries’ entire semiconductor budgets. Its next-generation “High-NA” EUV systems will cost $380 million each and enable 1.4nm chip production by 2027.

The strategic insight: ASML transformed from a supplier into a kingmaker. The company now determines which nations can access leading-edge semiconductors, making it more powerful than most central banks.

The Dragon’s Dilemma#

China faces an impossible trilemma. Beijing can pursue indigenous EUV development, accept permanent technological inferiority, or find alternative pathways that bypass traditional Moore’s Law scaling.

Option one seems most likely but faces crushing technical barriers. EUV development requires mastering dozens of frontier technologies — high-power lasers, ultra-precise optics, advanced materials science, and vacuum systems. Even if China achieves breakthrough progress, reaching commercial viability would require another 10-15 years and hundreds of billions in investment.

Chinese companies are already pivoting to alternative approaches. Huawei’s latest Mate 60 Pro smartphone uses 7nm chips produced through advanced packaging techniques that stack multiple older-generation processors. The approach works for mobile applications but cannot scale to high-performance computing.

The hidden reality: China’s semiconductor industry is fracturing into two separate ecosystems. Domestic companies will serve the Chinese market with “good enough” technology, while global leaders capture premium applications requiring cutting-edge performance.

The Next Battlefield#

The ASML chokepoint reveals something profound about modern geopolitics: control over critical technology infrastructure matters more than traditional military assets.

The Netherlands — a country with 17 million people — now wields more influence over global technology development than most nuclear powers. This dynamic will only intensify as artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and advanced manufacturing become central to economic competitiveness.

Other chokepoints are emerging across the technology stack. Applied Materials controls plasma etching equipment. KLA Corporation dominates inspection systems. Lam Research leads chemical vapor deposition tools. Each represents a potential pressure point in the new cold war.

What to Watch#

Monitor these specific signals to track how the semiconductor chess game evolves:

ASML’s China revenue in quarterly earnings calls. Any uptick suggests sanctions are weakening or alternative supply channels are emerging. Current baseline: sub-€300 million per quarter.

Chinese EUV patent filings through the World Intellectual Property Organization. Track applications from Tsinghua University, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and SMEE (Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment Group). Meaningful progress would signal breakthrough development.

Dutch government statements on export license reviews. Prime Minister Mark Rutte has faced intense Chinese diplomatic pressure. Any softening of restrictions would immediately boost ASML’s stock price and destabilize U.S. technology policy.

TSMC’s capital expenditure guidance. The company plans $40-50 billion in 2024 capex, with roughly 20% allocated to EUV systems. Higher spending signals stronger demand from Apple, NVIDIA, and other AI chip customers.

Alternative lithography developments. Watch for announcements from Canon’s nanoimprint lithography division or breakthrough progress in directed self-assembly techniques. True alternatives to EUV remain 5+ years away, but early signals matter for strategic planning.

The semiconductor supply chain represents the new nuclear deterrent — not through explosive force, but through the quiet power to switch off an adversary’s digital economy. ASML sits at the center of this transformation, wielding influence that most Fortune 500 CEOs can only dream of achieving.
