ASML: The $200M Monopoly Controlling Tech's Future

March 29, 2026

The world’s most powerful monopoly isn’t Google, Amazon, or even Saudi Aramco. It’s a Dutch company most people have never heard of, and it just became the single point of failure for the entire digital economy. ASML Holding produces 100% of the world’s extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines — the $200 million devices that etch circuits onto advanced semiconductors. Without ASML, there are no cutting-edge chips. Without cutting-edge chips, there’s no AI revolution, no 5G networks, and no modern military systems.

The amateur sees this as just another tech company. The strategist recognizes ASML as the ultimate economic weapon — and the geopolitical chess match for its control is reshaping global power dynamics in real time.

The Chokepoint Nobody Talks About#

ASML shipped exactly 31 EUV machines in Q3 2024, down from 34 in the previous quarter. These aren’t just statistics — they’re the productive capacity of the future global economy. Each machine can produce semiconductors at the 3-nanometer node, the bleeding edge required for everything from iPhone processors to military radar systems.

Here’s the kicker: ASML’s monopoly isn’t accidental. It’s the result of a 20-year, $8 billion R&D investment that created technology so complex that replicating it would take competitors at least a decade — if they could even secure the necessary components. The EUV light source alone requires 50,000 laser pulses per second to create plasma hotter than the surface of the sun, contained in a vacuum chamber cleaner than outer space.

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) operates 140 of ASML’s EUV machines — nearly 60% of the global installed base. Samsung has about 50 machines, Intel roughly 20. China’s Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation (SMIC)? Zero. Not because they don’t want them, but because they can’t get them.

The Chess Match: White Forces Black to React#

The United States recognized ASML’s strategic importance before most analysts even knew the company existed. In 2019, the Trump administration quietly pressured the Netherlands to block ASML’s planned sale of EUV machines to SMIC — a $150 million deal that would have given China access to advanced chip manufacturing.

The pressure worked. The Dutch government revoked ASML’s export license, effectively cutting China off from the most advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment on the planet. But here’s where it gets interesting: the Biden administration has doubled down, implementing the October 2022 export controls that didn’t just block EUV machines — they prohibited ASML from servicing existing deep ultraviolet (DUV) machines in China.

The result: China’s semiconductor foundries are slowly degrading. Without regular maintenance and software updates from ASML, their DUV machines — which can produce chips down to about 7 nanometers — are becoming less reliable and productive over time. It’s technological strangulation by maintenance deprivation.

China is playing black on this board, forced into reactive moves. They’re pouring $143 billion into domestic semiconductor development through various state-backed funds, but they’re essentially trying to rebuild 30 years of Dutch innovation from scratch. Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment (SMEE), China’s national champion lithography company, can produce machines capable of 90-nanometer processes — technology ASML mastered in 2004.

The Motive: Why ASML Benefits from Controlled Scarcity#

The surface narrative suggests ASML is caught in the middle of a geopolitical conflict. The reality is more sophisticated: ASML benefits enormously from controlled scarcity and geopolitical tension.

Follow the money: ASML’s stock price has risen 400% since 2019, when the U.S. first intervened in the China sales. The company’s market capitalization now exceeds $300 billion — larger than Intel, the company that pioneered the semiconductor industry. Revenue hit €27.6 billion in 2023, up from €18.6 billion in 2021.

The scarcity isn’t just geopolitical — it’s engineered. ASML deliberately maintains tight production capacity, producing only about 55-60 EUV machines annually despite customer demand for over 100 units. This artificial constraint allows them to charge premium prices and maintain waiting lists that extend 2-3 years.

CEO Peter Wennink has been explicit about this strategy, stating in earnings calls that ASML will “grow capacity in line with market demand” — a euphemism for maintaining scarcity pricing. Each EUV machine costs between $200-300 million, and customers pay 50% upfront with the remaining balance upon delivery.

The deeper motive: ASML has become indispensable to both Western semiconductor manufacturers and Western governments. They’ve achieved the ultimate business position — too important to fail, too valuable to regulate, and too complex for competitors to replicate quickly.

Historical Parallel: The Zeiss Precedent#

This isn’t the first time a single company controlled a critical industrial technology. From 1900 to 1945, German optics manufacturer Carl Zeiss dominated precision lens manufacturing, controlling the production of everything from camera lenses to military rangefinders.

Zeiss’s monopoly shaped World War II in ways most historians underestimate. German U-boat periscopes, tank rangefinders, and aircraft bomb sights all depended on Zeiss optics. When Allied forces captured Zeiss facilities in 1945, they discovered technology so advanced that it took American and British manufacturers years to reverse-engineer.

The parallel is precise: ASML today occupies the same systemic importance that Zeiss held during the industrial warfare era. The difference is scale — semiconductors touch every aspect of modern life, from smartphones to nuclear weapons systems.

The lesson: Monopolies over critical industrial technologies become geopolitical weapons, whether the company intends it or not. Zeiss ultimately lost its dominance when American companies like Eastman Kodak invested heavily in competing technologies during the war. China is attempting the same strategy with ASML, but the technology gap is far wider than it was in the 1940s.

The Production Reality Nobody Mentions#

ASML’s EUV machines don’t just etch circuits — they enable Moore’s Law to continue. Without EUV lithography, semiconductor manufacturers hit a physical wall at about 10 nanometers. The machines use 13.5-nanometer wavelength light to create circuit patterns so small that they approach atomic scales.

The production bottleneck is intentional: ASML sources critical components from over 5,000 suppliers across 40 countries. The mirrors inside each EUV machine are made by Carl Zeiss (yes, the same company) and require six months to manufacture. The laser systems come from Trumpf, another German manufacturer. The photomasks are produced by a handful of companies in Japan and South Korea.

This supply chain complexity isn’t accidental — it creates dependencies that make the technology nearly impossible to replicate. China would need to recreate not just ASML’s technology, but the entire ecosystem of specialized suppliers.

TSMC’s dominance in advanced semiconductor manufacturing (92% market share for chips below 10 nanometers) exists because they secured early access to ASML’s EUV machines. They placed orders for 100+ machines back in 2016, when few analysts understood their strategic importance. Intel, meanwhile, struggled with EUV integration and fell behind in the technology race.

The Framework: Technological Sovereignty vs. Economic Efficiency#

The ASML situation reveals a fundamental tension in modern geopolitics: the conflict between technological sovereignty and economic efficiency.

Economic efficiency suggests that one company should produce EUV machines for the world — it’s a natural monopoly given the massive R&D requirements and technical complexity. This is the globalization model that dominated from 1990-2015.

Technological sovereignty demands that strategic technologies be produced domestically, even if it’s economically inefficient. This is the deglobalization model emerging since 2016.

China is betting $143 billion on technological sovereignty. The U.S. is betting on controlling choke points in the global supply chain. Europe (through ASML and the Netherlands) is betting on maintaining neutrality while benefiting from both sides.

The hidden insight: This isn’t really about semiconductors — it’s about artificial intelligence. The chips produced by ASML’s EUV machines are essential for training large language models and running AI inference at scale. Whoever controls advanced semiconductor manufacturing controls the pace of AI development.

What to Watch#

The ASML chokepoint will define the next phase of technological competition. Monitor these specific indicators:

ASML’s production capacity: Watch for announcements about expanding manufacturing beyond 60 EUV machines annually. Any significant capacity increase signals a shift from scarcity pricing to market share competition.

Dutch government policy: The Netherlands faces increasing pressure from both the U.S. and China. Watch for changes in export licensing rules, particularly around DUV machine maintenance and software updates.

China’s lithography breakthrough: Track Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment’s progress toward 28-nanometer capability. If they achieve reliable 28nm production, it reduces China’s dependence on ASML for mature node manufacturing.

Intel’s EUV strategy: Intel is building new fabs in Arizona, Ohio, and Ireland with heavy EUV machine deployment. Watch their success rates — Intel’s ability to compete with TSMC depends on mastering EUV manufacturing processes.

Alternative technologies: Monitor developments in directed self-assembly, nanoimprint lithography, and other potential alternatives to EUV. While none are currently viable for advanced nodes, technological breakthroughs could reshape the competitive landscape.

The company that most people have never heard of has become the fulcrum of global technological power. ASML didn’t choose this role, but they’re leveraging it masterfully — maintaining artificial scarcity while both superpowers bid for access to their machines. In the chess match between the U.S. and China, ASML isn’t a piece on the board. They’ve become the board itself.
