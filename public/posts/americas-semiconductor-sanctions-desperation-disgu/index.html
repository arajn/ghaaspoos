America's Semiconductor Sanctions: Desperation Disguised as Strategy

May 21, 2026

The rookie sees sanctions as economic warfare. The strategist sees them as an admission that America is playing catch-up in the race that defines the next century.

While Washington burns through diplomatic capital imposing ever-tighter restrictions on Chinese semiconductor access, Beijing is quietly rewriting the rules of the game. The October 2022 export controls that sent shockwaves through global chip markets? They weren’t just trade policy — they were a desperate gambit by a superpower watching its technological monopoly crumble in real time.

The numbers tell the real story. Despite three years of escalating sanctions, China’s semiconductor imports increased by 13.9% in 2023, reaching $349.4 billion. Meanwhile, U.S. chip equipment exports to China dropped 24% to $27.4 billion — but Chinese companies simply found new suppliers. The sanctions aren’t choking Beijing’s chip ambitions. They’re accelerating them.

The Chess Opening: How America Misread the Board#

The Biden administration’s semiconductor strategy rests on a fundamental miscalculation: that China would remain dependent on Western technology indefinitely.

When the Bureau of Industry and Security unveiled its sweeping October 7, 2022 controls, targeting everything from advanced logic chips (sub-14nm) to high-bandwidth memory and supercomputing systems, the thinking was clear. Cut off the technological oxygen, and China’s AI and military modernization would suffocate.

The amateur saw this as economic dominance. Washington controlled the critical chokepoints — ASML’s extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines, Applied Materials’ deposition tools, and TSMC’s advanced manufacturing nodes. China needed these technologies to compete in artificial intelligence, autonomous weapons, and quantum computing.

The strategist recognizes this as strategic overreach. By weaponizing America’s technological dependencies, Washington didn’t just cut China off from Western supply chains — it gave Beijing the political cover and economic incentive to build parallel ecosystems that could eventually challenge U.S. dominance entirely.

Consider SMIC, China’s largest contract chipmaker. When the Trump administration placed it on the Entity List in December 2020, the company’s revenue growth stalled at 24.8% for 2021. But by 2023, after absorbing the full impact of Biden’s enhanced restrictions, SMIC posted revenue growth of 34.3%, reaching $6.3 billion. The sanctions didn’t kill the patient — they forced it to evolve.

Follow the Money: Beijing’s $1.4 Trillion Semiconductor Moonshot#

While Washington focuses on restrictions, Beijing is writing checks.

China’s national semiconductor fund, known as the “Big Fund,” has deployed over $200 billion across two phases since 2014. But that’s just the visible tip of the iceberg. Local governments have launched complementary funds worth an estimated $600 billion, while state-owned enterprises have committed another $400 billion in chip-related investments through 2030.

The scale dwarfs anything attempted in the West. The U.S. CHIPS Act allocated $52 billion over five years. Europe’s European Chips Act targets €43 billion through 2030. China’s combined public and private semiconductor investment pipeline approaches $1.4 trillion — nearly 30 times America’s commitment.

This isn’t just about money. It’s about time horizons. While U.S. semiconductor policy gets reshuffled every election cycle, China’s approach spans decades. The Communist Party’s 14th Five-Year Plan (2021-2025) explicitly targets 70% domestic chip self-sufficiency by 2025. Early indicators suggest they’re ahead of schedule.

Take memory chips, where China has made stunning progress. Yangtze Memory Technologies Co. (YMTC) was shipping competitive 232-layer NAND flash memory by late 2022 — just four years after receiving its first major state funding. When the Biden administration blacklisted YMTC in December 2022, cutting off its access to U.S. equipment suppliers, the company had already secured over $24 billion in domestic orders.

The pattern repeats across subsectors. ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) achieved 17nm DRAM production in 2023, breaking South Korea’s duopoly in advanced memory. Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment (SMEE) delivered its first 28nm lithography scanner in early 2024 — not yet competitive with ASML’s latest generation, but closing the gap faster than most analysts predicted.

The Historical Parallel: Japan’s Semiconductor Rise and Fall#

China’s current position mirrors Japan’s semiconductor trajectory in the 1980s, but with crucial differences that suggest a different outcome.

In 1989, Japan controlled 53% of global semiconductor production, threatening American technological leadership. The U.S. response was swift and devastating: the 1986 U.S.-Japan Semiconductor Trade Agreement imposed punitive tariffs and forced Japan to guarantee American companies 20% of its domestic market.

Japanese semiconductor giants like NEC, Toshiba, and Hitachi never recovered. By 2020, Japan’s global market share had collapsed to just 10%. South Korea’s Samsung and SK Hynix, along with Taiwan’s TSMC, inherited Japan’s position.

But China in 2024 is not Japan in 1986. Japan was a military ally constrained by security treaties and dependent on American protection. China is a strategic rival with nuclear weapons, the world’s second-largest economy, and explicit ambitions to reshape the global order.

More importantly, Japan’s semiconductor industry was export-dependent and integrated into U.S. supply chains. China is building parallel supply chains designed to operate independently of Western control. When Washington restricts access to EUV machines, Beijing accelerates development of alternative lithography techniques. When U.S. companies cut off advanced chips, Chinese firms redesign their systems to use older, domestically available semiconductors.

The sanctions haven’t created Japanese-style capitulation. They’ve catalyzed Chinese-style self-reliance.

The Real Winners: Playing Both Sides of the New Iron Curtain#

While Washington and Beijing engage in technological warfare, a handful of companies are quietly profiting from both sides.

Samsung has emerged as the clearest winner. The South Korean giant has increased its Chinese market share as U.S. competitors face restrictions, while simultaneously securing massive subsidies from the Biden administration to build new fabs in Texas and New York. Samsung’s semiconductor revenue hit $63.7 billion in 2023, up 18% despite the global downturn.

Dutch equipment maker ASML represents the most fascinating case study in strategic neutrality. While ASML has complied with U.S. pressure to restrict EUV machine exports to China, it has dramatically increased sales of older deep ultraviolet (DUV) systems that remain unrestricted. ASML’s China revenue jumped 39% in 2023 to $2.9 billion, even as political tensions escalated.

The company’s approach reveals the sanctions’ core weakness. Current restrictions target cutting-edge technologies (sub-7nm logic chips, high-bandwidth memory above 18nm), but China’s domestic market demands hundreds of billions of dollars worth of mature semiconductors for automobiles, industrial equipment, and consumer electronics. ASML is capturing that demand while Washington focuses on preventing Chinese access to technologies that represent perhaps 5% of the global chip market.

Meanwhile, China’s restrictions on critical mineral exports are creating their own winners. Rare earth processing company MP Materials saw its stock surge 127% in 2023 as investors bet on Western supply chain diversification. Lithium producers Albemarle and Livent have signed multi-billion-dollar partnerships with Chinese battery makers, hedging against potential future export restrictions.

The Endgame: Who Controls the Next Technological Epoch?#

The amateur focuses on today’s chip sanctions. The strategist sees tomorrow’s technological architecture.

China’s semiconductor strategy isn’t ultimately about achieving parity with Taiwan or South Korea in manufacturing. It’s about controlling the next generation of computing architectures before they become strategically critical.

Beijing is making massive bets on post-silicon technologies. Chinese researchers published 46% of global quantum computing papers in 2023, according to the Australian Strategic Policy Institute. State-owned enterprises have committed over $100 billion to quantum research through 2030, targeting quantum advantage in cryptography and drug discovery before Western competitors establish dominance.

Similarly, China’s neuromorphic computing investments — chips designed to mimic brain functionality — totaled $18 billion in 2023, compared to just $3.2 billion in combined U.S. government and private funding. If neuromorphic chips prove superior for AI inference, China could leapfrog entirely over the current GPU-centric paradigm that NVIDIA dominates.

The pattern extends to manufacturing. While the U.S. restricts China’s access to EUV lithography, Chinese researchers are pioneering alternative approaches: maskless lithography using electron beams, nanoimprint lithography for certain applications, and even exploring quantum lithography techniques that could theoretically achieve atomic-scale precision.

The goal isn’t to play America’s game better. It’s to change the rules entirely.

What to Watch#

Monitor China’s 2025 self-sufficiency metrics. Beijing has committed to 70% domestic chip self-sufficiency by December 2025. Current estimates suggest they’re at roughly 35-40% across all categories. Watch for announcements around Chinese New Year 2025 — traditional timing for major policy updates.

Track YMTC and CXMT revenue figures. These memory chip champions represent China’s most advanced semiconductor capabilities. If their combined quarterly revenues exceed $8 billion by mid-2025, it signals successful displacement of Samsung and SK Hynix in China’s domestic market.

Follow U.S. equipment company earnings calls. Applied Materials, Lam Research, and KLA Corporation report China revenue as a separate line item. If their combined China exposure drops below 15% of total revenue, it indicates successful supply chain diversification — or Chinese self-sufficiency.

Watch for Chinese quantum computing announcements. Beijing has promised a “quantum advantage” demonstration by 2027 — a computer that performs specific calculations exponentially faster than classical systems. Success would represent the first major technological breakthrough where China leads rather than follows.

Monitor rare earth export restrictions. China controls 87% of global rare earth processing. Any expansion of export controls beyond gallium and germanium to include neodymium or dysprosium would signal escalation to a new phase of technological warfare.

The semiconductor cold war isn’t about chips. It’s about who writes the rules for the next century of human technological development. Based on current trajectories, the answer may surprise Washington.
